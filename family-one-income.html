One-income household planner

Compare one expected monthly take-home income with your planned monthly household outgoings. Use the result to find a budget surplus or shortfall, not as a yes-or-no decision about leaving a job.

Use monthly net pay, not annual or gross salary. A positive remainder is not a complete affordability assessment. Benefits, emergency needs and long-term goals require a separate review. Inputs stay in your browser.

Prepare the two monthly inputs

  1. Enter expected monthly take-home pay after taxes and payroll deductions in the proposed one-income arrangement. If insurance or other deductions would change, update the estimate rather than reusing the old paycheck.
  2. Enter monthly household outgoings, including bills, debt payments and planned savings not already deducted from pay. Convert less frequent costs to a monthly allowance.
  3. Read the monthly remainder and its 12-month equivalent. A negative number is a shortfall, not a calculation error. Try a realistic higher-expense scenario as well.

Avoid double-counting: for example, a retirement contribution already deducted from the net-pay input should not also appear as an expense unless you intend an additional contribution.

Worked example: $6,500 in and $5,000 out

$6,500 of monthly take-home pay minus $5,000 of monthly outgoings leaves $1,500 a month. If both amounts stayed unchanged for 12 months, the annualized remainder would be $18,000. With $7,000 of outgoings instead, the result would be a $500 monthly shortfall, or $6,000 over 12 months.

The annualized figure is not guaranteed savings. Unplanned spending, an income interruption or an omitted cost can change it.

Methodology and a fuller household review

Monthly remainder = monthly take-home income − monthly household outgoings. Annualized remainder = monthly remainder × 12. Values are displayed to the nearest cent; intermediate calculations are not rounded.

The CFPB recommends comparing realistic spending with monthly take-home pay, including savings and less frequent costs. Review several months of actual transactions rather than only an ideal budget. Read the CFPB spending assessment guide.

  • Allow for annual insurance bills, repairs, medical costs, school expenses, travel and other irregular needs.
  • Consider changes to childcare and commuting costs, health coverage, other employee benefits and retirement contributions or employer matching.
  • Keep emergency reserves, debt obligations and long-term retirement goals separate from this simple monthly subtraction.
  • If pay varies, a monthly average can hide weeks when bills arrive before income. This tool does not build a cash-flow calendar.

Taxes, investment returns, inflation, benefit replacement, lost employer contributions and the timing of a career change are not calculated. This tool neither recommends a job decision nor determines whether a household can safely live on one income.
